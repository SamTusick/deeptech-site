Rocket Payload
Challenge

Two student payloads. One real rocket flight. Design a small experiment that tests a technology with a possible real-world application, and fly it on the FGCU DeepTech rocket.

Student-led · advisor-supported · FGCU reviewed

Student teams selected
2
Payload component award, per team

Target launch
Nov 302026 or later

Selected teams receive $500 for payload components, technical support, an integration path, and the chance to test a minimum viable product under real flight conditions — with real data to guide the next step. Rough ideas are welcome; no finalized design is required.

The approach

Think like a startup: prove the riskiest assumption.

Don't build the final product. Build the smallest experiment that answers the most important question. One clear question, one useful experiment.

Who can enter

Every major can contribute to a flight experiment.

  • FGCU students from any major
  • Preferably 1–6 students per team
  • No aerospace, coding, electronics or engineering experience required

Almost any technology can become a payload.

The flight must teach you something that matters beyond the competition. These are prompts, not boundaries — propose a different idea if the flight can test it.

Keep it simple

A winning payload is simple enough to finish.

You receive no extra points for complexity. Buy what exists; build only what proves your idea.

SensorMeasure the effect
MCU / computerRun the logic
BatteryPower the test
MemorySave the data
EnclosureHold it securely

Arduino, Raspberry Pi, ESP32, commercial sensors, GPS, cameras, 3D-printed parts and open-source software are all fair game.

If shortlisted

Tell the story in five minutes.

A 5-minute presentation plus about 5 minutes of questions. Polish matters less than clarity and execution.

  • What problem are you solving?
  • What is your idea?
  • Why is it useful or interesting?
  • What will the rocket help you test?
  • Can your team build it before launch?
Judging

Strong ideas win only when they can be executed.

Safety and fit are checked first. Qualifying proposals are then scored on six criteria. A simple experiment that answers a valuable question can beat a more complex proposal.

  • Innovation25%
  • Impact + applications20%
  • Feasibility before launch20%
  • Quality of experiment15%
  • Simplicity + smart reuse10%
  • Team + funding strategy10%
Safety

Disclose unusual features early.

You aren't expected to arrive knowing rocket safety. You are expected to tell us what your payload contains. Flag these items:

  • Liquids or chemicals
  • Pressurized containers
  • Heaters or powerful batteries
  • Radio transmitters or lasers
  • Magnets or sharp components
  • Moving or external mechanisms

The payload must not interfere with

  • Flight computer
  • Parachutes
  • Tracking system
  • Rocket structure
  • Propulsion system

FGCU safety and research-compliance review is required before hardware or flight. Use commercially or publicly available, or approved, components — no controlled technical data or restricted technology.

Ground testing

Your rocket flight should not be the first test.

Ground testing makes flight data trustworthy. Before flight, confirm that:

  • The battery lasts long enough
  • Memory saves the data
  • Components remain attached
  • Sensors and cameras record
  • Software starts reliably
  • The payload survives shaking
After the flight

Recover the payload and data. Explain what worked, what surprised you, what you learned, and what you would test next.

A result that disproves your assumption can still be a successful experiment.

Students should retain the ability to develop their ideas beyond the competition, subject to applicable FGCU policies and any separate agreements.
